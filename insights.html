AI in schools is four technologies, not one — here's what actually works

Most debates about AI in education collapse into "is the chatbot good or bad." The research says the real question is who is designing the implementation, and against what evidence — and how well it adapts to each learner.

Ali FarshadfardFounder, GaiaLink Intelligence SystemsJuly 20268 min read

Every school district conversation about AI seems to boil down to the same binary: is the chatbot good or bad for kids? After digging into the last two years of published research on AI in education — RCTs, national surveys, and pilot post-mortems — I don't think that's the right question. The evidence points somewhere more specific: outcomes depend almost entirely on how a school implements AI, not on whether it uses AI at all.

Here's what the data actually shows, and what it means for anyone building or buying AI tools for a Canadian classroom.

Key takeaways
  • "AI in schools" is really four distinct technologies — generative co-pilots, adaptive engines, agentic systems, and predictive analytics — each with its own evidence base.
  • Only 19% of teachers work at a school with a written AI policy, even though 60% already use an AI tool for their work.
  • Structured, teacher-framed AI tutoring has real trial evidence behind it: a 2025 Harvard study and an Edo State, Nigeria RCT both found large learning gains.
  • Personalization — not automation — is the actual point: it's the first realistic shot at Bloom's 1984 "two sigma" tutoring effect at classroom scale.
  • Most AI initiatives in schools fail from missing policy and missing measurement, not from the technology itself.

Four technologies, not one

"AI in education" usually gets treated as a single tool — a chatbot bolted onto a lesson plan. In practice it's at least four distinct capabilities, each doing a different job in a school:

Generative co-pilots
Adaptive engines
Agentic systems
Predictive analytics

A district that evaluates "AI" by piloting one chatbot is really only testing about a quarter of what the category can do. Content generation, personalized pacing, multi-step task execution, and pattern-detection in school data are four separate design problems with four separate evidence bases.

The governance gap is the real leadership gap

A national survey of over 2,200 U.S. public school teachers, published by Gallup and the Walton Family Foundation in 2025, found that 60% had used an AI tool for their work that school year, and 32% were using one at least weekly.1 Set that against a separate finding from the same survey: only 19% of teachers work at a school with a written AI policy, and 68% say they've received no formal training on how to use these tools at all.1

That's the gap that matters most: usage is already mainstream, and formal readiness is not. Governance isn't paperwork layered on top of the technology — it's the difference between a school that can say what "acceptable use" means and one that finds out the hard way.

The technology is rarely the bottleneck. The absence of a plan is.

The evidence is more specific than the hype

Not all classroom AI use is equally supported by research, and the gap between "strong evidence" and "no evidence" is wider than most pitch decks suggest:

Strong evidence

A 2025 Harvard randomized trial (published in Nature Scientific Reports) found a structured, Socratic AI physics tutor produced larger learning gains than an in-class active-learning lesson, with students finishing faster and reporting higher engagement.2 A separate RCT in Edo State, Nigeria found six weeks of teacher-guided generative-AI tutoring produced gains equivalent to roughly two years of typical schooling.3

Emerging

Feedback and marking support at scale, and administrative time savings — teachers who use AI weekly report saving an average of 5.9 hours a week, close to six working weeks over a school year.1 Effects on assessment quality and early-warning analytics are still being studied.

Caution

Unstructured, open-ended chatbot use with no pedagogical scaffolding — the pattern researchers call cognitive offloading. Independently reviewed evidence for many popular consumer AI tutoring products is still thin or pending; treat vendor claims skeptically until an independent study backs them.4

This mirrors a much older finding: Benjamin Bloom's 1984 "two sigma" research showed one-to-one tutoring pushed the average student two standard deviations above a conventional classroom — to roughly the 98th percentile.5 The open question ever since has been how to get close to that effect at classroom scale. Structured, teacher-framed AI tutoring is the first technology with credible trial evidence that it can.

Integrity is a design problem, not a policing problem

When most students already have a tutor in their pocket, detection software can't carry academic integrity on its own — and chasing detection tools is a losing race against the technology it's trying to catch. The more durable fix is to design assessments that reveal real learning under AI conditions: assess process alongside product, bring back students' actual voice through in-class defenses or drafts, use authentic performance tasks, and make AI use something students disclose rather than hide.

The teacher isn't being replaced. The job description is.

Content deliverer becomes learning architect. Marker of scripts becomes designer of feedback loops. The parts of teaching AI genuinely can't touch — motivation, relationships, judgment, safeguarding, classroom culture — become the whole point of the role rather than what's left over after marking.

And the time AI hands back has to be reinvested deliberately: with a global shortfall of an estimated 44 million teachers projected by 2030, UNESCO's own analysis is blunt that recovered hours matter far more if they go toward retention and mentorship, not just more volume.6

Why most school AI initiatives quietly die

Five patterns recur across the pilots that stall: picking a tool before naming the problem, running pilots that never formally end or expand, leaving policy undefined, giving teachers no protected time or training, and — most common of all — measuring nothing. A result that was never checked against a baseline isn't evidence. It's an anecdote.

Purpose without proof is just a speech. Pilots without policy are a scandal waiting to happen. Run the sequence in order and skip none of the steps.
